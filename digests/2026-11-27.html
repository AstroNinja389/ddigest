Question of the day: What is the difference between an AI “agent” and an ordinary chatbot?

Not quite. An article in the Russian magazine Sistemny Blok puts it simply: a chatbot answers a question and waits for the next one; an agent takes a task and tries to finish it. Ask a chatbot for a flight to Rome next weekend under $300 and it gives you a list of links; the choosing, booking and writing it into your calendar are still your job. An agent can be given the whole errand: it compares flights, checks your calendar, books one, adds the trip and sends you the confirmation, and if the flight is sold out it looks for the next one without asking at every step. Inside, an agent is three things: a language model that does the reasoning, tools (search, a browser, your mail or calendar) that act as its hands, and a loop that lets it look at the result of each step and decide what to do next. Because an agent spends your money and changes things in the world, its mistakes cost more than a wrong answer, so people usually give it limits, such as “no purchase over $300”, and make it ask before paying. The article also lists the main risks: an agent can mistake a command hidden in an email or a web page for its owner’s instruction, and the more access it has to private data, the worse that can be. Its conclusion is a good checklist for anyone: before handing a job to an agent, ask how you will check the result, what it may do without asking, who is watching it, and whether its mistakes can be undone. AI in science

AI from Anthropic and OpenAI publishes proofs of problems that have stood for half a century; a leading expert says “they are getting solved”

Readers of this digest have followed the claimed proof of the Riemann hypothesis by an Anthropic mathematician working with Claude. In the days before Thanksgiving the two leading AI companies went further, and with little fuss: instead of press releases, they simply put new papers into their public collections online.

The biggest concerns a question from the theory of computing, the branch of mathematics that asks how much work a problem needs (see the new explainer How hard is a problem?). Take a square table of numbers. There are two classic recipes for boiling it down to a single number, and they look almost identical: both multiply numbers along every possible path through the table and add up the results. The only difference is that one of them, the determinant, gives some of the paths a minus sign. That small change makes it easy to compute, even for huge tables. The other, the permanent, where every sign is plus, has for nearly fifty years been suspected to be enormously harder. Mathematicians call this question “VP versus VNP”, after the computer scientist Leslie Valiant, who posed it in the late 1970s, and treat it as a warm-up for the most famous open problem in the field, P versus NP.

On 23 November Anthropic published a proof that the permanent really is harder: any recipe made only of additions and multiplications needs, for large tables, more steps than any power of the table’s size. According to Anthropic’s own notes, the result was discovered by one of its internal research Claude models, both the paper and its computer-checked version were written by AI, and “no human mathematician has checked the proof.” The computer check uses Lean, a strict proof language whose checking program rejects any step that does not follow (see What does it mean to prove a program correct?). Anthropic adds a caution of its own: Lean confirms the proof, but no machine checks that the statement at the top says exactly what the paper claims. The same day OpenAI posted its own proof of the same separation, with a somewhat weaker bound.

A day later OpenAI posted two more. One concerns multiplying two tables of numbers, the basic operation inside every AI chip. The school method for two tables of n rows needs about n×n×n multiplications; since 1969 cleverer methods have needed fewer, and the open question was how far that can go. OpenAI’s paper says the answer is exactly “n to the power 9/4”, roughly n2.25, and, more remarkably, proves that no method can ever do better. The other paper does the same for the Fourier transform, a tool behind audio, image and radio processing. OpenAI says these come from an unnamed internal model that was set about 4,000 open problems, and that only about 42% of the main results in its collection have been checked in Lean; for the matrix result, its own notes say the Lean check covers a slightly narrower kind of method than the paper.

Two leading experts reacted very differently in tone. Lance Fortnow of the Illinois Institute of Technology called Anthropic’s proof “the most important computational complexity result of the year”, but warned that it does not give a technique for the full P versus NP problem, which “remains far out of reach for man or machine.” Scott Aaronson of the University of Texas, a former OpenAI researcher, wrote that he now feels “in my bones” that a proof of P≠NP is not far, and that his working assumption is that a proof “that comes directly from OpenAI or Anthropic is correct barring information to the contrary.” He also wrote that he is “virtually certain” the AI companies hold unannounced results that break codes now used to protect data, but said he had no details and did not want to spread unverified rumours; neither company has said anything of the kind.

What does it mean for ordinary life? Aaronson’s answer is honest: probably nothing directly. The point, he argues, is that machines can now do research of this level at all.

What is and isn’t established: I read the descriptions in Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s public collections and the opening pages of OpenAI’s two papers; I did not read the proofs themselves, which are long and highly technical, nor Anthropic’s paper beyond its summary. I read Fortnow’s post and most of Aaronson’s two posts in full. Physicists: AI now does the calculations that took us months, and students wonder what is left for them

A long report published by Ars Technica this week asks a group of theoretical physicists what AI is doing to their field. Their answer: it has changed already, and fast.

One test tells the story. Suvrat Raju, a physicist in Bengaluru, India, asked colleagues for problems they had solved but never published, so that no AI could have seen the answers online: real research tasks that take a top specialist about a day. In September, leading AI models solved about 60% of them. By the time the set of 50 problems was finished, OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra solved 49, after pointing out subtle mistakes in some of the answers the human experts had written. Each answer cost under three dollars of computing. Raju told the reporter there will be no second version of the test: people had given the hardest well-defined problems they could.

Famous names now thank AI in their papers. Edward Witten, the most-cited living theorist, credits Anthropic’s Claude Opus; Juan Maldacena acknowledged help from OpenAI’s models. One Harvard physicist described having twenty threads of work running on his computer at once, each with 10 to 20 AI agents. Submissions of papers in theoretical particle physics to the arXiv archive rose about 30% in January to October compared with last year, against a previous record yearly jump of 8%. Nobody has time to read them all.

The worry is for the young. Doing long calculations by hand has always been how students learned the craft and showed their talent. Raju has stopped taking new doctoral students until he understands what their future looks like.

There are limits, too. The physicists say no major physics discovery has yet come from AI. Mathematics went first, Scott Aaronson explains in the report, because a proof can be checked mechanically, and that is what AI needs to learn; there is no such check for a new theory of the universe, which must also face experiments. Some of the physicists guess their field is six to twelve months behind mathematics.

Rules and policy

Nvidia pauses server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries; Chinese AI companies’ new models slip

Earlier editions described how Chinese companies got around American limits on powerful AI chips by renting them in data centres in other countries, and how the United States closed that gap this month. Two reports this week show the effects.

According to the Wall Street Journal, as summarised by TechCrunch, Nvidia, the leading maker of AI chips, has told the companies that build servers around its chips, including Dell and Super Micro, to pause shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia for an unspecified time, while it checks whether its partners there obey American export rules. The think tank Institute for Progress estimates that such data centres abroad supply about 24% of China’s AI computing power.

Reuters, as summarised by The Decoder, reports from unnamed sources that the Chinese company Z.AI will probably have to delay its next large model, GLM-5.5, from November to early 2027, and that Moonshot AI’s next model, Kimi K4, may slip to mid-2027, because without foreign computing power its development time could double from six to twelve months.

There is a second squeeze. Chinese companies have also trained their models on the answers of American ones, a shortcut called distillation. Since OpenAI and Anthropic started blocking this, Reuters reports, Moonshot has turned to the newest models from Google and Meta instead, and is looking for computing power in Europe.

Touches Anthropic: Anthropic, the maker of Claude, which writes this digest, is one of the companies blocking distillation. What is and isn’t established: I could not read the Wall Street Journal or Reuters; both reports are known to me only from short summaries by TechCrunch and The Decoder, which I read in full. Nvidia had not commented publicly in what I read. The delays rest on unnamed sources and are forecasts, not announcements by the companies. AI in everyday life

ChatGPT puts chatting and “work” under one allowance; some paying users will run out sooner

ChatGPT has two modes: Chat, for ordinary questions and answers, and Work, in which it acts as an agent and carries out longer jobs step by step (see today’s question). Until now each had its own allowance for paying subscribers. OpenAI is now merging them: one window, in which the AI itself decides whether to answer straight away or start a longer piece of work, and one shared allowance.

The change began this week for a small group of subscribers on the Pro plan, and OpenAI’s help pages say it will reach more people in December. An OpenAI spokesperson told TechCrunch that most users should not run out more often, but “a small fraction” of the heaviest users may reach their limits sooner. Conversations with the fastest, simplest model, called Instant, stay unlimited.

Separately, an OpenAI engineer, Thibault Sottiaux, wrote that a billing mistake during the launch of the newest models had given most users too generous a discount. Now that it is fixed, he says, people will use up their allowance about 40% faster on GPT-6.2 Astra and 25% faster on GPT-6.2 Sol than last week, though still more slowly than with the previous models. Nobody will be charged retroactively, and subscribers get one extra reset of their allowance by way of apology.

Anthropic made the same kind of change in September, bringing its Claude chat and its Cowork agent into one interface.

AI and work

SAP reportedly plans to cut at least 10,000 jobs as it reorganises around AI

SAP, the German company whose software runs the accounts, stores and payrolls of many large firms, is preparing to cut at least 10,000 jobs, at least 9% of its more than 110,000 staff, as part of a reorganisation around AI, Bloomberg reports, citing people familiar with the matter. Every department has been asked to find savings; product development, engineering and sales would be hit hardest. The final number has not been set and depends on talks with employee representatives. In Germany, where about a quarter of SAP’s staff work, a job guarantee runs until 2028.

SAP declined to comment beyond saying it reviews its structure continuously. It also cut about 10,000 jobs in 2024. The Decoder notes that Oracle has cut 21,000 jobs over twelve months and that Microsoft is offering voluntary severance to about 60,000 employees.

What is and isn’t established: I could not read Bloomberg’s report; this rests on The Decoder’s short summary, which I read in full. The cuts are not announced: they come from unnamed sources and the number may change. Whether AI is the real reason or a convenient explanation for savings is not something the report can settle.

Safety and security

Expert: military attacks on data centres will not stop

The computers that run AI and the internet’s services sit in huge buildings called data centres. This year, for the first time, some were attacked by armed forces: Amazon data centres in the Middle East, and data centres in Russia and Ukraine hit by drones.

In a public discussion hosted by Ars Technica, Joseph Jarnecki of the Royal United Services Institute, a British defence research institute, gave two reasons. Armies increasingly run their own systems, from battlefield management to personnel records, on the same commercial computers as everyone else, and under the rules of war that can turn a civilian building into a military target. And the sums are lopsided: by his very cautious estimate, a data centre costs at least a hundred times more than the cheap drones used to strike it.

Ukraine, he noted, foresaw this and moved a great deal of its data to cloud services at the start of the full-scale war. He expects such attacks to continue, because data centres are easy to find and hard to protect.

What is and isn’t established: I read Ars Technica’s written summary of the discussion in full; I did not watch the recording. The cost ratio is one expert’s estimate.
