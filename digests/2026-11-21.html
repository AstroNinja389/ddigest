Question of the day: Were the first computers built to break codes?

To a large extent, yes, says an article from the Museum of Cryptography published in the Russian magazine Sistemny Blok. During the Second World War, the British codebreaking centre at Bletchley Park needed to read messages sent by the German high command on a cipher machine called Lorenz. A Cambridge mathematician, Bill Tutte, worked out how that machine was built without ever seeing one, from intercepted messages and a German operator’s mistake of reusing the same settings. To do the enormous number of comparisons needed every day, the engineer Tommy Flowers and his colleagues built Colossus, an electronic machine full of radio valves; it first ran in December 1943, and its decoded messages helped the planning of the Normandy landings in June 1944. Earlier, Polish mathematicians led by Marian Rejewski had used mathematics and special machines to break the better-known Enigma. The article’s point is that for decades afterwards computers were used mainly to break and analyse ciphers, not to encrypt anything, because they were huge and expensive; and that much of this history was forgotten because it stayed secret. Most of the ten Colossus machines were destroyed after the war, and the article notes that a method for exchanging secret keys, published in 1976, had been found earlier at Britain’s intelligence agency and was declassified only in 1997. The article dates from March 2024.

AI and science

A mathematician at Anthropic says that he and Claude have proved the Riemann hypothesis; on the same day, a human-only rival proof appeared

The Riemann hypothesis is often called the most important unsolved problem in mathematics. Put forward by the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann in 1859, it is, at heart, a precise claim about how the prime numbers (2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and so on) are scattered along the number line. Thousands of published results assume it is true, and there is a one-million-dollar prize for a proof.

On Friday 20 November Levent Alpöge, a mathematician on Anthropic’s technical staff, posted a short note on his personal website. “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis,” he writes, adding that the method also settles a broader version, the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. He describes guiding the work towards the right setting, after which Claude “far outpaced” him near the finish line, including suggesting a key step.

Unusually, he has not released a paper that people can read. The proof is published only as computer code in Lean, a program that checks every step of a mathematical argument mechanically (see what “formal verification” means). It is enormous: Kevin Buzzard of Imperial College London told Al Jazeera it is about 17 million lines, and that for now “we don’t even know why the theorem is true”. According to TechCrunch, several mathematicians, among them Andrew Sutherland of MIT, say they have run the code themselves and the computer accepts it. That is a strong sign, but not the end: experts still have to make sure the code states the Riemann hypothesis correctly and does not quietly assume anything extra, and no human has yet read the argument through.

Alpöge says he found the proof “late Sunday night”, then messaged Anthropic’s chief executive, Dario Amodei, to ask about announcing it. He acknowledges that the timing, right after Anthropic’s arrival on the stock exchange, invites a cynical reading, and says that reading is false. Anthropic itself has posted nothing about it on its news page.

The same day, Ravi Vakil of Stanford University, the president of the American Mathematical Society, posted on his blog a separate draft proof of the generalized hypothesis, more than 900 pages long, by Alessandro Masullo, who began the work as a graduate student at Stanford under the mathematician Brian Conrad. Vakil calls it “all human, with no AI”. Masullo writes that the work took nearly 12 years, that he was advised to release it unfinished “in light of the current climate”, and that he knows of “several glitches”. Within hours, Vakil added that several people had reported problems, found with the help of AI, which must be resolved before the proof could be accepted.

The business of AI

Anthropic’s shares slide almost back to their sale price on their second day, in a bad week for AI stocks

When a company first sells its shares to the public, it is called an IPO, an “initial public offering”. Anthropic did this this week, at $83 a share, which valued it at about $1.5 trillion. On Friday 20 November it announced that the sale had closed: about $93.5 billion of shares were sold, about $91.6 billion of it going to Anthropic, plus a further $26 billion of shares sold privately at the same price to big technology partners, AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and the chip maker TSMC among them.

Thursday, the first day of trading, went well: according to Fortune, the shares opened at $111.50, briefly valued the company at more than $2 trillion and closed at $97.17. Friday went badly. The price fell more than 14% during the day to $83.05, just five cents above the sale price, before recovering to around $87 by midday.

Fortune puts this down to a wider fall in technology shares, worries about the troubles at the chip maker Nvidia, and the results of the American midterm elections. Many outlets described a sharp fall in AI-related shares over the week, but I could not read those reports, so I give no figures for the wider market.

A share price moving on its second day says little about whether a company is doing well; it reflects what buyers and sellers think at that moment.

Safety and security

A security researcher planted traps in two million AI requests and found that some were read by people who should not have seen them

When you type something into an AI service, it often does not go straight to the company that made the model. Middlemen called “routers” pass requests on to whichever of many companies is running that model at the moment. Some routes carry a label promising “zero data retention”: nothing you send is kept.

HD Moore, chief research officer at the security company Dragos, tested those promises. From July to November 2026 he sent about two million requests through such services, each containing a unique web address or fake password, a “canary”. Nobody has any reason to open such an address or use such a password unless they are reading the request, so each time one was used, he knew someone had looked. In a report published on 19 November, he says he found evidence that at least three AI providers had been broken into, that resellers on one marketplace were viewing and keeping customers’ requests, and that privacy promises were not kept. In the largest case, a couple of hundred fake keys for Amazon’s cloud service were taken. “A privacy flag doesn’t help when the attacker is already inside,” he writes.

On 21 November the news site PrivacyAffairs reported that OpenRouter, one of the biggest routers, had emailed customers that requests they sent to models from MiniMax, a Chinese AI company, “may have been read”: test addresses had been opened 40 to 130 hours later. OpenRouter says the problem lies with MiniMax and the companies that passed requests to it, not with OpenRouter, and it has removed the “zero data retention” label from those routes. MiniMax’s explanation, as relayed, is that a content-checking system had been switched on.

The practical lesson is old: do not paste passwords, bank details or anything truly private into an AI chat, whatever the label says. For more, see AI and cybersecurity.

Rules and policy

Cursor, a popular AI coding tool, tells US lawmakers it may stop using Chinese AI models

Cursor is a widely used program in which AI helps people write software; it has been owned by SpaceX since August. Its own coding model, Composer 2, was built on top of Kimi K2.5, a model from the Chinese company Moonshot AI. Such Chinese models are often released as open weights: anyone can download and adapt them for free, which is why many Western companies use them.

According to CNBC, which saw the document before publication, a report by two committees of the US House of Representatives, on Homeland Security and on China, says Cursor “informed the Committees” that it is considering phasing out Chinese-made open-weight models because of geopolitical risk. The committees also spoke with Airbnb and DoorDash about their use of Chinese models.

The report recommends a national American open-weight programme, so companies have home-grown alternatives, and a ban on Chinese-developed AI models in federal government computer systems and contracts, extending a ban on DeepSeek made public in October. A report’s recommendations are not law.

What is and isn’t established: I read CNBC’s article, but not the committees’ report itself. Cursor, SpaceX and DoorDash did not respond to CNBC, and Airbnb declined to comment, so Cursor’s plans are known only from the report.

Progress and testing

Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Mini, a free model that runs on a laptop by going over its own work three times

Thinking Machines, a start-up founded by OpenAI’s former chief technology officer Mira Murati, released Inkling 2 Mini on 20 November as open weights, free to download and use. It is small enough, about 20 gigabytes, to run on a Mac with 32 GB of memory.

Its trick is a “looped” design: for every word it produces, it runs the middle part of the model three times, a little like rereading a sentence before answering, so a small model gets more thinking out of the same parts. The company says it now leads all free models of its size on several coding tests (see what benchmarks are).

The independent testers Artificial Analysis give it the same overall score as Alibaba’s Qwen3.8 27B, while doing each test task about six times more cheaply and four times faster; they also note that it writes about 38% more words to get there.

What is and isn’t established: I read the company’s announcement, its model page and Artificial Analysis’s article. The coding results are the company’s own; the overall score is Artificial Analysis’s.

Data centres

Study: America’s largest data centres emit almost as much carbon as Denmark

Data centres are the vast buildings full of computers that run AI (see what a data centre is). According to Semafor, Harvard researchers writing in the journal Nature Sustainability estimate that 403 of the largest American data centres used almost 2% of the country’s electricity, mostly from fossil fuels, and emit about 25.7 million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year, close to Denmark’s total. Sites in Iowa, Ohio and Virginia account for about half.

Semafor says this is the first peer-reviewed estimate since the AI boom began, that it is not a full inventory, and that the true figure is probably higher.

What is and isn’t established: I read Semafor’s report of 17 November. I could not read the study itself (the journal asked for a sign-in) or the New York Times’s report, so the figures are Semafor’s account of the study.
